## Global Events and Energy Markets

The 2022 energy crisis demonstrated how interconnected global oil markets are and how quickly geopolitical events can impact consumer energy costs.

## The Timeline: Crisis Unfolds

**February 24, 2022: Russia invades Ukraine**

In the early morning hours, Russian forces crossed the Ukraine border. Within hours, crude oil jumped $10 per barrel (8%), the largest single-day move in a decade. Gasoline prices began their climb—not overnight, but inexorably.

**Why did energy markets react instantly?**

Russia and Ukraine together account for ~3% of global crude oil supply and ~12% of wheat. But the real concern was supply *uncertainty*. With Russian ports under potential blockade and Ukraine's production threatened, traders feared:

- Months of supply interruption 
- Potential NATO-Russia escalation cutting off Russian energy to Europe
- Demand destruction in Europe as energy costs spiked
- Cascading economic damage

On March 7, 2022, WTI crude hit $130/barrel—the highest nominal price ever recorded (exceeding 2008's $145). U.S. gasoline prices averaged $4.13/gallon by March 11—a 38-year record and 26% higher than one month prior.

## Why Ukraine Mattered: The Energy Map

**Russia's Energy Leverage**

Russia is the world's 2nd-largest oil exporters (after Saudi Arabia) and 3rd-largest producer. More critically, **Russia supplies 10% of global crude oil and 30% of European natural gas**. 

The U.S. has limited exposure to Russian crude (historically ~3–4% of U.S. imports; by 2022, it had fallen to 2% due to earlier sanctions). But the U.S. energy markets are *global*. When Russian oil is cut off from Europe, European buyers bid for Middle Eastern, African, and North American oil, raising prices worldwide.

**Ukraine's Role**

Ukraine itself produces minimal crude (15,000 bbl/day—less than 0.1% of global supply) but was strategically positioned as a transit route for Russian gas to Europe. The invasion:

- Threatened pipeline infrastructure
- Created political uncertainty over gas contracts
- Forced Europe to scramble for alternative LNG supplies

**The Contagion Path**: Russia supply disruption → Europe scrambles for oil → bidding up global oil prices → U.S. oil prices rise → U.S. gasoline prices spike → household fuel costs jump.

## The Crisis Spreads: March–May 2022

As crude held above $100/barrel, several factors intensified the crisis:

**1. Refinery Outages Compound Supply Shock**

- March 17: Chevron's Richmond, CA refinery (largest PADD 5 supplier) shut down unexpectedly.
- April 6: Colonial Pipeline (carrying 45% of East Coast fuel) experienced a ransomware cyberattack, causing a 5-day disruption.
- May 2: Tesoro's Anacortes refinery (Pacific Northwest) had a power outage.

These outages, normally manageable, hit during a global supply crisis. U.S. refinery utilization dropped to 90%—its lowest in months—and inventories began depleting.

**Impact on consumers:**
- National average reached $5.016/gallon on June 14, 2022 (nominal record; highest in real terms was still 2008).
- West Coast (PADD 5): $6.30–6.50 (tight supply + regional refinery outages).
- Midwest (PADD 2): $4.70–4.90 (buffered by Gulf Coast supply via pipelines).

**2. OPEC+ Inaction, Then Action**

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC+, led by Saudi Arabia and including Russia) typically responds to price spikes by increasing production, stabilizing prices. But in 2022:

- Through March–April: OPEC+ made no significant production increases, despite crude above $100.
- In May: OPEC+ actually announced a *production cut* of 100,000 bbl/day—a bizarre move into a supply crisis.
- The rationale: Maintain prices high to maximize revenue and punish the West for sanctions on Russian oil.

This politicization of energy markets was dramatic and infuriating to U.S. policymakers. Saudi Arabia and OPEC+ were effectively supporting higher prices and helping Russia through inaction and production cuts.

**3. U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) Release**

In response, President Biden authorized the largest-ever SPR release: 180 million barrels over 6 months (1 million bbl/day).

- This added 3–4% extra supply to the market.
- Crude fell from $130 to ~$80–90 by December 2022.
- But SPR inventory fell to the lowest level since 1987—a tradeoff between immediate relief and long-term strategic security.

The SPR release was a macro policy move affecting national-scale crude prices, but regional gasoline prices remained high due to refinery constraints and PADD-specific supply limits.

## The Household Impact: Real Numbers

**Fuel Cost Surge**

- Average U.S. household drive: 12,000 miles/year ÷ 25 mpg = 480 gallons/year.
- In 2021 (pre-crisis): $480 gallons × $3.10/gallon = $1,488/year average.
- In 2022 (crisis peak): $480 gallons × $4.75/gallon (average during spike) = $2,280/year.
- **Incremental cost**: $792/year, or **$66/month extra**.

For a household spending $2,000/month on essentials (rent, food, utilities, transportation), a $66/month fuel increase is 3.3% of total spending—meaningful belt-tightening.

**Regional Disparity**

- **West Coast households**: Paid an extra $0.80–1.20/gallon above U.S. average for 3–4 months. A 12,000-mile driver paid $384–576 extra vs. national average.
- **Midwest households**: Paid ~$0.30 extra vs. national average during the sharpest phase, adding $144/year.
- **Poorer households** (lower income, longer commute): Felt 5–8X worse because fuel is a larger% of their spending.

**Knock-on Effects**

Higher fuel costs cascaded:

- **Delivery and shipping**: UPS, FedEx surcharges increased 10–15%; consumer shipment costs and e-commerce prices rose.
- **Airline costs**: Jet fuel costs tripled; airline ticket prices rose 20–40% summer 2022. Families' vacation plans got more expensive or postponed.
- **Food prices**: Diesel for agricultural equipment and transport spiked; food prices rose 5–8% through 2022–2023. Grocery bills climbed faster than fuel costs alone would suggest.
- **Heating costs**: In winter 2022–2023, natural gas and heating oil costs spiked. Northeast households saw heating bills double.

The energy crisis became an *inflation crisis*, hitting everything priced or powered by energy.

## Policy Responses and Market Dynamics

**1. Biden's Strategic Response**

- SPR release (above).
- Pressure on OPEC+ to increase production (ignored until December 2022).
- Waiver of ethanol blending rules (RVP waiver) to increase fuel supply during summer, normally prohibited to reduce smog.
- Calls for windfall profit taxes on oil companies (politically popular but economically controversial—no legislation passed).

**2. Industry Response**

- Refineries ramped to 97–99% utilization (near maximum operational capacity).
- Oil majors (ExxonMobil, Chevron) shifted production away from Russia to Saudi, Iraq, and UAE crudes.
- Pipeline operators prioritized throughput; no new refinery capacity came online (new refineries take 5–10 years).

**3. Consumer Behavior Shifts**

- Miles driven declined 1–2% nationally (people consolidated trips, delayed travel).
- EV and hybrid vehicle purchases surged; buyers suddenly cared about MPG again.
- Transit ridership increased in major cities.
- Remote work adoption remained elevated, reducing commute miles.

## The Decline: June–December 2022

Starting in June 2022, several factors eased the crisis:

**1. Demand Destruction**

As prices stayed high, consumers drove less. U.S. gasoline consumption fell 5–7% during summer 2022 vs. prior year—unusual, since summer is peak season. Higher prices literally priced people out of discretionary driving.

**2. Recession Fears**

By July, Fed rate hikes and inflation concerns triggered stock market declines and recession worries. Energy prices typically fall on recession fears (lower economic activity = lower energy demand). Crude fell from $130 to $90.

**3. Geopolitical Resolution (Partial)**

By September 2022:
- Ukraine supply situation stabilized; Russian blockades of grain/energy were partially lifted.
- Europe secured alternative LNG suppliers (Australia, Norway, Qatar).
- Fears of WWIII receded; risk premium on oil deflated.

**4. OPEC+ Finally Acts (Unexpectedly)**

On October 5, 2022, OPEC+ announced a production *cut* of 2 million bbl/day—a shock decision that defied Biden's pressure and threatened to re-ignite prices. However, the announcement itself spooked markets (was it a sign of confidence in higher prices? Or fear of recession?), and oil fell further, ending under $80/barrel by November.

By December 2022:
- Crude: $70–75/barrel
- U.S. gasoline: $3.40/gallon average (still high vs. pre-crisis $3.10, but down 30% from peak)
- Refinery inventories: Rebuilding
- Consumer behavior: Returning to near-normal (but EVs still more popular)

## The Lasting Imprints

**What Changed After 2022?**

1. **Energy Independence Focus**: U.S. federal policy shifted to drilling, permitting, and LNG export expansion to reduce European dependence on Russian gas. This accelerated shale production permits and LNG infrastructure projects.

2. **Europe's Energy Shock**: Europe faced a 2022–2023 crisis scarier than the U.S. — natural gas prices hit $300/MWh (vs. $3 pre-crisis). The continent rapidly deployed renewables, gas storage, and LNG terminals. Russia's energy leverage is permanently weakened.

3. **PADD 5 Fragility**: The West Coast's regulatory isolation and supply constraints became visible to policymakers. Calls to relax fuel specs or fast-track refinery capacity emerged (though little legislative action followed).

4. **Market Architecture**: Traders realized that geopolitical risk premiums on oil are sharper and more volatile than in the 2000s–2010s. Derivatives markets for oil became more complex; hedging more expensive.

5. **EV Adoption Acceleration**: 2022 catalyzed a decade-long shift away from ICE vehicles. EV sales hit 9% of new U.S. car sales in 2023, up from 3% in 2021. Long-term, this reduces oil demand.

## The Counterfactual: What If Russia Hadn't Invaded?

This is an economist's thought experiment. If the invasion had not occurred:

- Crude would likely have traded $70–90/barrel (supply/demand balanced, modest geopolitical premiums).
- U.S. gasoline: $3.20–3.50 average (no spike above $5).
- Household fuel costs: $1,536–1,680/year (vs. $2,280 actual).
- Inflation: Lower by 1–2 percentage points.
- SPR: Still full (would have been drawn down for other purposes, or remained idle).
- OPEC+: Would likely have maintained stable production, not cut in October.

The 2022 crisis was heavily exogenous—rooted in geopolitics, not demand/supply fundamentals. That makes it instructive for households: energy prices are subject to sudden shocks beyond economic rationality.

## Lessons for the 2020s

**1. Energy Markets Are Geopolitical**

Oil is priced in a global marketplace, and there's no "national" gasoline market insulated from global events. Even when the U.S. is energy-independent on paper, we pay global prices.

**2. PADD Fragmentation Amplifies Costs**

The West Coast's isolation meant it suffered worse (PADD 5 hit $6.30; PADD 2 hit $4.90). Refinery outages matter. Regulatory specs matter. Geography matters.

**3. Supply Resilience Is Undervalued**

The SPR release and demand destruction were the two things that actually eased the crisis. Policymakers learned that strategic reserves and a flexible population are the real hedges against supply shocks.

**4. Renewables and EVs Are Macro Buffers**

Countries and households that shifted to EVs, solar, wind, and gas-heated homes less dependent on crude insulated themselves. The IRA (Inflation Reduction Act) investments in EVs and renewables are, in part, a response to 2022.

**5. Transparency Matters**

## The Bottom Line

The 2022 energy crisis showed that geopolitical shocks to energy are real, immediate, and hit household wallets hard. A single event (invasion) triggered a $792 annual cost increase for the median American household and $2+ billion in aggregate economic disruption.

The crisis is over—crude has cooled, refineries are running steady, and geopolitical risk premiums have deflated. But the *fragility* remains. Russia can still cut supply; another OPEC+ split is possible; refinery capacity is still constrained.
