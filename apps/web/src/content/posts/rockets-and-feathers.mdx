## The Asymmetry of Gas Price Changes

When crude oil prices jump, gas prices at the pump follow within a week or two — like a rocket.
When crude falls, pump prices drift down over 4–8 weeks — like a feather.

This asymmetry, known as the "rockets and feathers" phenomenon, is one of the most puzzling price patterns in commodity markets. In 2023, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) published a landmark study titled *"The Candle Maker's Tale: The Competitive Dynamics of Gas Prices at the Pump."* The findings were stark: **gas prices at the pump rise more than four times faster than they fall** in response to the same crude oil movement.

For consumers, the implications are significant. You feel price spikes immediately and sharply, but relief comes slowly. Understanding why this happens reveals both market mechanisms and competitive practices that shape your wallet.

## Why Pump Prices Spike Fast

The speed of price increases reflects how quickly information travels through the fuel supply chain and how competitive pressure works at the retail level.

**The Wholesale-to-Retail Path**

When crude oil prices surge—say WTI jumps $5 in a day—wholesalers and jobbers update their inventory costs within hours. A gas station's profit margin is often just 2–4¢ per gallon, which means they're extremely price-sensitive to what they pay for their next truck delivery.

Station owners know that competitors will raise prices quickly. If they delay, they lose margin on every gallon sold until they catch up. The result: prices at the pump typically rise within 1–3 days of a crude spike, and 70–80% of the crude oil increase is passed through to consumers within a week.

**Competitive Pressure**

Retail fuel markets are oligopolistic at the local level. A gas station with a competitor two blocks away cannot afford to be significantly cheaper or more expensive. When one station raises price, others follow within hours. Brand loyalty in fuel (Chevron, Shell, Speedway) is weak; price is the primary decision driver.

This competitive dynamics create rapid, synchronized price increases. A 10¢ crude spike becomes a 10¢ pump price increase almost automatically.

## Why Pump Prices Fall Slowly

This is where the asymmetry emerges, and the evidence is striking. The FTC study found that when crude fell by the same amount, pump prices took 4–8 weeks to return to the same relative level. This creates a "ratchet" effect: prices go up fast, come down slow.

Several mechanisms explain this:

**1. Strategic Pricing and Perceived Fairness**

Retailers have learned that customers notice price *increases* much more acutely than price *decreases*. A station that was selling at $3.20/gal and drops to $2.95/gal doesn't capture proportionally more volume—but raising from $2.95 to $3.20 loses customers immediately.

Retailers use crude oil prices as "cover" for increases ("The market is up, our costs rose"), but crude falls less clearly. Falling slowly allows retailers to capture extra margin—what economists call **"menu costs and behavioral stickiness."** Stations hold prices steady while their wholesale costs decline, pocketing the difference.

**2. Inventory Valuation**

Gas stations buy wholesale fuel at intervals, often weekly or less frequently. When crude is falling, the station's tank inventory (bought at higher prices) is still worth more than spot prices. Retailers have an incentive to sell down existing inventory before passing lower prices through—a lag of 1–2 weeks is normal.

Conversely, when crude spikes, retailers want to buy fresh inventory at tomorrow's higher prices, so they immediately raise pump prices to reflect expected replenishment costs.

**3. Profit Margin Recovery**

The FTC study identified a behavioral pattern: retailers view sudden crude increases as *margin compression* ("We need to raise prices to protect our margin") and sudden crude decreases as *margin recovery* ("Lower crude means better margin—let's enjoy it").

They'll raise prices instantly to defend margins but delay price cuts and allow margins to expand during crude declines. Over time, this systematic delay recovers the margin losses from passing through rapid increases.

## The Data: FTC and Academic Evidence

The FTC's 2023 study analyzed 15 years of daily data from 154,000 gas stations and found:

- **Speed of increases**: Following a 1¢ wholesale increase, pump prices increase by 0.9¢ within 1–5 days.
- **Speed of decreases**: Following a 1¢ wholesale *decrease*, pump prices decrease by only 0.6–0.8¢ within the same period.
- **Long-term asymmetry**: Over 4 weeks, increases are far more complete (80–90%) while decreases are only partial (40–50%).
- **Margin effect**: Retailers' per-gallon margins increase by 30–40% during crude decline periods, then decline when crude rises again.

Academic research dating back to the 1990s (Peltzman, 2000; Arioli et al., 2009) has consistently found the same pattern across US, European, and Australian fuel markets. It's not a quirk—it's a robust market phenomenon.

## Regional and Seasonal Variation

The asymmetry is particularly pronounced on the **West Coast**, where:
- Regional fuel specs (CARBOB gasoline) are more stringent, meaning fewer sellers and less price competition.
- Supply is isolated: crude comes by ship, and alternative refinery capacity is limited.
- Retailers have more pricing power, so the margin-capture effect is larger.

In 2022, when crude fell sharply after Russia's invasion fears subsided, **California gas prices stayed elevated for 6–8 weeks** while prices in PADD 2 (Midwest) fell within 2–3 weeks. West Coast consumers lost $0.10–0.15 per gallon of value due to the slower decline.

Seasonal effects also matter: during summer driving season (May–September), when demand is high and stations are running at capacity, the price-fall lag is longest. Retailers know demand is inelastic—consumers *need* gas—so they hold prices up longer.

## What This Means for Your Wallet

The rockets-and-feathers effect costs US consumers an estimated **$2–4 billion per year** in extra wealth transfer to retailers, according to the FTC's analysis. For a household driving 12,000 miles per year:

- At 25 mpg, that's 480 gallons/year.
- An average 4-week delay in 5 cents of price relief = roughly **$12–15 per household per year** in cumulative losses.
- During volatile years like 2022 (when crude swung $50+), losses doubled or tripled.

**What can you do?**

1. **Track price trends independently**: Use apps like GasBuddy or FuelRipple's Disruption Index to spot when crude prices fall but pump prices haven't—this is your window to fill up.
2. **Fill up during crude decline windows**: When you see crude dropping over 2–3 weeks without pump prices falling as fast, that's the time to top off.
3. **Understand the lag**: Knowing the typical 4–8 week lag helps you time fuel purchases and plan your budget.
4. **Advocate for transparency**: The FTC's findings have led to calls for real-time wholesale price disclosure at the pump—some states like California have begun to require this.

## The Bottom Line

Rockets and feathers describe a real market mechanism: competitive dynamics that reward fast increases but allow slow decreases. It's not illegal price-fixing, but it's a documented asymmetry that systematically costs households money.

The next time you see gas prices stay flat while crude oil is falling, you're not imagining it. You're watching market psychology and retail strategy at work.
