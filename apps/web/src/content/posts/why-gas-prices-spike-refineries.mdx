## The Price Spike That Crude Oil Can't Explain

Everyone knows crude oil drives gasoline prices. When OPEC cuts production or a geopolitical crisis erupts, you can watch the national average tick upward in near-lockstep with the barrel price. That relationship is well understood, widely covered, and accounts for roughly half of what you pay at the pump.

But crude oil doesn't explain the *other* kind of price spike — the sudden, regional jolts where gas jumps 30 or 40 cents in a single week while the rest of the country barely notices. Those spikes have a different cause, one that gets far less attention: refinery utilization. Specifically, what happens when a refinery unexpectedly goes offline in a region that has no slack to absorb the loss.

## One Refinery Goes Down. Prices Jump 30 Cents.

On February 18, 2015, an explosion and fire struck ExxonMobil's refinery in Torrance, California — the third-largest refinery in Southern California. The facility's fluid catalytic cracker, which represented roughly 22% of the region's total catalytic cracking capacity, went offline without warning. Within five days, wholesale spot prices for California-grade gasoline (known as CARBOB) in Los Angeles jumped 22 cents per gallon. By late April, retail prices in Los Angeles had climbed 63 cents per gallon in just three weeks, while the national average moved only 20 cents over the same period. By July, the wholesale premium for Los Angeles CARBOB over the national benchmark had ballooned to more than $1.30 per gallon.

That wasn't a crude oil story. Global oil markets barely flinched. It was a refinery story — and understanding why it played out the way it did requires understanding something most consumers never think about: how tightly American refineries run, and how little room exists to absorb a disruption.

## What Refinery Utilization Actually Means

Refinery utilization is the ratio of crude oil actually being processed at a refinery to its total operable capacity. A refinery running at 90% utilization is processing 90% of the crude it theoretically could.

That number matters enormously for a simple reason: the U.S. no longer has significant spare refining capacity. According to the EIA's landmark 2007 study on refinery outages, utilization averaged around 78% in 1985, a time when outages barely registered in product prices because the system had ample slack. Between 1985 and 1995, demand grew while capacity stayed flat, pushing utilization up to 92%. Since the mid-1990s, American refineries have routinely operated above 90% during peak summer driving months, and fleet-wide annual averages rarely climb much beyond 95% even in good years, because planned maintenance windows and seasonal demand troughs pull the average down.

The practical implication is that the refining system runs hot. During peak summer demand, there is very little spare capacity sitting idle, waiting to be called upon if something goes wrong. This is a fundamentally different situation from crude oil, where strategic reserves, OPEC spare capacity, and globally traded cargoes can often absorb a disruption over days or weeks. Refined gasoline, by contrast, is a regional product — it must meet local fuel specifications, travel through specific pipeline and marine networks, and arrive at terminals near the point of consumption. When a refinery producing gasoline for a particular region goes down, the replacement supply can't just materialize from a global spot market overnight.

## Why There's No Slack in the System

To understand why a single refinery outage creates such disproportionate price effects, it helps to appreciate three characteristics of the American refining system.

First, the number of operating refineries has been declining for decades. Consolidation has been the dominant trend since the 1980s: California alone went from 40 refineries in 1983 to about 13 by 2025. The remaining facilities are larger and more efficient, but there are fewer of them, meaning each one represents a larger share of regional supply. When one goes offline, it takes a proportionally larger bite out of the market.

Second, refinery maintenance is deliberately scheduled for low-demand periods — typically spring and fall — and refiners stockpile product ahead of planned shutdowns so their supply obligations can still be met. The market "expects" these outages and prices adjust gradually if they adjust at all. Unplanned outages, by contrast, hit without warning. As the GAO documented in its 2009 analysis of refinery outages across 75 U.S. cities, planned shutdowns generally had no significant effect on wholesale prices, whereas unplanned outages were consistently associated with price increases. Of roughly 1,100 unplanned outages the GAO evaluated between 2002 and 2008, 99% were associated with wholesale price jumps of up to 32 cents per gallon, and 75% with increases under 6 cents. That might sound modest, but those are averages across all regions — and they mask the extreme outcomes that occur in geographically isolated markets.

Third, and most critically, not all gasoline is interchangeable. The U.S. is a patchwork of different fuel specifications. California's CARBOB is one of the most stringent blends in the country and can only be manufactured by refineries equipped to produce it. A Gulf Coast refinery running conventional reformulated gasoline cannot simply redirect that product to Los Angeles to cover a shortfall — it would not meet California's air-quality requirements. Academic research has confirmed that the price impact of refinery outages is significantly larger for these specialized "boutique" fuels than for conventional gasoline. The less fungible the product, the harder it is to replace from alternative sources when supply is disrupted.

## The PADD System: Why Geography Is Destiny

The U.S. divides its petroleum markets into five Petroleum Administration for Defense Districts, or PADDs, a system dating to World War II that remains the primary framework for tracking fuel supply and demand. Understanding the PADD map is essential to understanding why the same-sized refinery outage can have vastly different price consequences depending on where it happens.

**PADD 3 (Gulf Coast)** is the country's refining powerhouse. Stretching from New Mexico to Alabama, it holds roughly half of total U.S. refining capacity and consistently produces far more gasoline than the region consumes. That surplus flows outward via the Colonial Pipeline to the East Coast (PADD 1), through pipelines northward to the Midwest (PADD 2), and via tanker to export markets. Because of this massive surplus and robust pipeline infrastructure, a single refinery outage on the Gulf Coast is cushioned by an enormous pool of neighboring capacity. Prices may twitch, but alternative supply typically reaches affected markets quickly.

**PADD 5 (West Coast)** is the opposite case and the clearest illustration of how refinery geography creates price vulnerability. The mainland West Coast is bracketed by the Pacific Ocean to the west and the Rocky Mountains to the east, with no major product pipelines connecting it to the Gulf Coast refining hub. Within PADD 5, supply and demand centers around Seattle, San Francisco, and Los Angeles are themselves loosely connected — product moves between them mainly by marine tanker along the coast, a process that takes days, not hours. On top of that geographic isolation, California's CARBOB specification limits which facilities worldwide can even produce a compatible replacement product. When the EIA analyzed the Torrance outage, it identified four potential resupply routes: drawing down regional inventories, shipping from other West Coast refineries via coastal tanker, bringing product from the Gulf Coast on Jones Act–compliant vessels, and importing from overseas (primarily South Korea or India). Each of those options involved lead times measured in weeks, not days. Gulf Coast CARBOB, shipped by coastwise vessel, would take three to four weeks to reach Southern California. Asian imports took about four weeks from order to delivery. During that lag, the market has no choice but to allocate scarce existing supply through price — which is exactly what a 30- to 60-cent spike represents.

**PADDs 1, 2, and 4** (East Coast, Midwest, and Rocky Mountain) each have their own supply dynamics, but the general principle holds: the more connected a region is to surplus refining capacity via pipeline or short-haul marine infrastructure, the more cushioned it is against any single outage. The Rocky Mountain region (PADD 4) is relatively small and somewhat isolated, making it more sensitive to local disruptions than the East Coast or Midwest.

## The West Coast as a Worsening Case Study

The structural vulnerability of the West Coast market is not a static problem — it is getting worse. A series of refinery closures and conversions over the past several years has steadily reduced the region's production capacity. Marathon closed its Martinez refinery in 2020. Phillips 66 converted its Rodeo refinery near San Francisco from crude processing to renewable diesel production in early 2024. Phillips 66 then announced the permanent closure of its 139,000-barrel-per-day Wilmington refinery in Los Angeles, with operations ending in late 2025. And Valero filed notice to cease refining at its 145,000-barrel-per-day Benicia refinery in the Bay Area by April 2026.

Together, the Wilmington and Benicia closures alone represent about 17% of California's refining capacity and 11% of total West Coast (PADD 5) capacity — even though they account for less than 2% of national capacity. The EIA has projected that West Coast retail gasoline prices will rise in 2026, even as prices fall in every other region of the country, specifically because of these capacity losses. The agency projects the West Coast average could reach approximately $4.19 per gallon, while the national average dips below $3.00.

The region's growing dependence on imported gasoline — West Coast gasoline imports hit a record above 210,000 barrels per day in late May 2025 — introduces additional fragility. Imports from Asia take weeks to arrange and deliver, meaning they cannot respond quickly to sudden demand surges or further unplanned outages. California's new minimum inventory law, enacted in late 2024, is an attempt to build a buffer against exactly this kind of supply fragility, but it represents an acknowledgment that the market itself no longer has sufficient slack to self-correct in real time.

California also supplies roughly one-third of Arizona's gasoline and nearly 90% of Nevada's, meaning that reduced California production ripples outward into neighboring states that have no refineries of their own.

## What This Means for Consumers — and What to Watch

The core lesson is that gasoline prices are not just a function of global crude oil markets. They are equally a function of local refining capacity, regional infrastructure connectivity, and the fungibility of the specific fuel blends your area requires. Two people in different parts of the country can face dramatically different price trajectories from the same underlying crude oil market, purely because of the refining and logistics infrastructure between the barrel of oil and their local gas station.

For consumers tracking fuel costs, the variables to watch extend well beyond the Brent or WTI crude benchmark. Refinery utilization rates, unplanned outage reports, regional inventory levels, and the PADD-level supply balance are all signals that can telegraph a price spike days or weeks before it shows up at the pump.
